How Social Security Works

A plain-language guide to disability benefits

What Social Security looks at, how work credits work, and what to expect if your claim goes to a hearing.

1. Are you "disabled" under Social Security's rules?

Social Security uses a strict definition. You must have a medical condition that keeps you from doing substantial work and that has lasted, or is expected to last, at least 12 months or result in death. Partial or short-term disability does not count.

2. Work credits (for SSDI)

You earn work credits by working and paying Social Security taxes. You can earn up to four credits each year, and the amount of earnings needed for one credit goes up slightly every year.

  • Most people need 40 credits, with 20 earned in the last 10 years before becoming disabled.
  • Younger workers need fewer credits.
  • If you stopped working years ago, your coverage may have run out. Your "date last insured" sets the deadline for proving you became disabled.
  • You can check your credits in your my Social Security account.

SSI does not use work credits. It looks at your income and resources instead.

3. What to expect at a hearing

If your application and reconsideration are denied, you can request a hearing before an Administrative Law Judge. Hearings are informal compared with a courtroom and usually last under an hour.

  • You, your representative, and often a vocational expert take part, in person, by phone, or by video.
  • The judge asks about your health, treatment, daily activities, and past jobs.
  • The vocational expert answers questions about what kinds of work a person with your limits could do.
  • A written decision arrives by mail, usually weeks or months later.

4. If you are denied again

You can ask the Appeals Council to review the judge's decision, and after that, file in federal court. Each step has its own deadline, usually 60 days. See Practice Areas & Stages for the full path.

Tip: Keep going to your doctors and follow your treatment plan. Gaps in treatment are one of the most common reasons claims are denied.

This page is a general overview, not legal advice. Rules and dollar amounts change; the Social Security Administration publishes current figures.
