How Social Security Disability Works

Medical eligibility

Social Security decides whether you have a medical condition that keeps you from doing substantial work, and that has lasted or is expected to last at least 12 months. Your medical records are the main evidence, so regular treatment and complete records matter.

Work credits (SSDI)

You earn credits by working and paying Social Security taxes, up to four per year. Most adults need about 40 credits, with 20 earned in the last 10 years before disability began. Younger workers can qualify with fewer. You can check yours in your my Social Security account.

Age and work history

For workers 50 and older, Social Security considers whether you could move to other work given your age, education, and skills. This is why a long career in physical labor can strengthen a claim when you can no longer do that work.

Hearings

If your claim is denied twice, you can ask for a hearing before an Administrative Law Judge. The judge reviews your records and may question you and a vocational expert. Preparing your testimony ahead of time is one of the most useful things you can do.

Appeals

After a hearing, you can ask the Appeals Council to review the decision, and then file a lawsuit in federal court. Each step has its own deadline, usually 60 days.
